Abstract—Dynamic grid simulations depend on properly calibrated parameters to accurately model physical grid response
to hypothetical disturbances. We showcase a novel method to
calibrate parameters, hence reducing epistemic uncertainty in
grid models. The proposed method relies on simulation-based
inference and neural networks (NN) as classical physics-driven
inverse problems are highly non-linear and intractable for power
grid forward models. Contrary to classical NNs that provide
point estimates, the designed NNs provide a companion variancecovariance to point estimates providing a measure of uncertainty
about the output calibration. Additionally, as operational grids
spans across thousands of buses, we propose a statistical method
to subsample data in order to accelerate NN training in large
configurations. We demonstrate varying levels of success on grid
examples, and provide a sensitivity analysis method to determine
whether grid parameters are easily estimable.
Index Terms—grid simulation, parameter calibration, uncertainty reduction, neural networks, simulation-based inference,
contingency analysis

I. I NTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
With the increasing capability of dynamic grid simulation
models, it is becoming more desirable to model larger grids
with more configurable parameters to reflect physical grids as
closely as possible. Such simulations allow contingency analysis testing of grid resilience under hypothetical disturbances
such as line faults, generator failure, or sudden connection
or disconnection of large loads. However, in order for these
models to accurately reflect the true grid response, their
parameters must be realistically calibrated.

The objective of epistemic uncertainty reduction in this
context is to estimate grid parameters of interest (e.g. rotor inertia), with minimal uncertainty, based on observations of that
physical grid (e.g. voltage angle over time). These estimates
should be calibrated such that simulations of a digital-twin grid
using these calibrated parameter values resemble observations
of the physical grid as closely as possible. The uncertainty
quantification (UQ) task depends on properly understanding
and modeling relationships between the grid-parameter values,
the simulation output, and physical measurements of the grid.
Parameter calibration in dynamic electrical grid models is of
significant interest in the literature, due to regulatory requirements to periodically update generator stability models [1].
One significant approach is calibrating generator models using
of Kalman filter, ensemble Kalman filter, or Unscented Kalman
filter methods with PMU observations near the generator [e.g.,
1, 2, 3]. While [3] reports standard errors on the parameter
estimates, the uncertainty is rarely of interest in these works.
Various methods are used to determine which model parameters are identifiable from a given PMU observation, to
avoid wasted computational effort attempting to calibrate nonidentifiable parameters. Sensitivity analyses (SA) fill this role,
for example trajectory-based SA [2] and Gramian-based SA
[4]. Acilan and Gol [5] go further, defining a “distractor”
parameter as one that is identifiable but already correctly
calibrated, and developing a NN-based method to flag such
parameters. In a Bayesian setting, a distractor parameter may
still have its posterior uncertainty reduced relative to its prior,
even if the posterior mean estimate is not updated.
Dynamic simulation parameters have been estimated by
posteriors conditioned on PMU observations using several
Bayesian methods including Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) [4], conditional variational autoencoders [6],
polynomial chaos expansion and Markov chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) [7], and hierarchical Bayesian inference and importance sampling [8]. Machine learning-based approaches have
also been used, including double deep Q-learning for load
model calibration [9]. Outside of dynamic models, statistical
methods have been used to infer parameters of electrical loads,
such as to determine when large loads are turned on/off using

observed power consumption data [10, 11, 12].
Multiple solutions to the parameter calibration problem have
been a concern to [2, 5], where multiple distinct sets of
parameter values can result in equally good fits to observed
data, possibly due to collinearity of the parameters’ effects.
Our Bayesian approach accommodates this possibility through
the posterior correlation in the multivariate Gaussian estimate.

Simulation-based inference (SBI) is a class of statistical
techniques that replace intractable likelihood or loss function
calculations with a trainable surrogate: a complex model is
treated as a black-box simulator, run many times over parameter draws, and a machine-learning model is trained on the simulated (parameter, data) pairs to approximate parameters given
new observations. This enables inference for complex highly
non-linear systems like power grids whose likelihoods/loss
functions cannot be written down. SBI includes methods such
as ABC, but is a broader category of methods that all attempt
to solve the same fundamental problem [13].
GridKit [14, 15] is a next-generation grid simulator that uses multiple advanced analysis methods such as dynamic constrained optimization and
partitioned numerical integrators to perform phasor dynamics
simulations on grids up to thousands of buses in size. Our epistemic uncertainty reduction workflow employs GridKit to simulate phasor dynamics and parameter responses on the ACTIVSg200 test case, a 200-bus synthetic grid representing central Illinois with 40 GENROU (round-rotor synchronous generator) units. This case is modified from the Texas A&M Electric Grid Test Case repository [24] for GridKit compatibility and serves as a practical testbed for calibrating rotor inertia parameters on larger-scale, realistic systems.
II. M ETHODS
A. Multivariate Gaussian Loss NN
Our approach to simulation-based inference follows ideas
presented in [16], [17]. We first simulate grid parameters, θ,
from their prior distribution, then we simulate observations, y
using GridKit for its deterministic part and from our approximate observation distribution, p̃(y|θ) for its stochastic part.
Then, we train a NN N using these simulated y ↦→ θ pairs
so that given an observation, we can estimate the parameters
that produced it. We extend [16], [17] by estimating not just
point estimates with squared-error loss, but posterior mean
and covariance with a Gaussian loss. Specifically, we optimize
the NN with data y as inputs Nw : y ↦→ (θ, Σ) weights w.
The multivariate Gaussian output and loss compile the output
values of the network into a mean vector and covariance
matrix, which represent the prediction and its uncertainty,
respectively. If the network is predicting p parameters, then it
will output p + p(p + 1)/2 values, p of which form the mean
vector, µ, while the remaining values form the lower-triangular
Cholesky factor of the covariance matrix, L, where Σ = LL⊤ .
The covariance matrix Σ is symmetric positive definite, hence
estimating its Cholesky factor enables to reduce the amount of
estimated coefficients while ensuring the positive definiteness
of the reconstructed covariance. Then for a training input, y,
and output, θ, the loss is calculated as

The input data y have a multivariate time series structure,
consisting of voltage magnitude and phase measurements on
several buses over a period of time surrounding an induced
fault (see Sect. II-B1). The voltage angle as a variable can
be problematic due to wrap-around values near 2π and 0.
To resolve this, the angle is decomposed into sin and cos
components, creating two continuous series containing all
the original information in the angle. The NN thus begins
with several convolutional layers. Each convolution layer has
a kernel size of 3, uses a stride of 2 to down-sample the
time dimension by a factor of 2, and increases the number
of channels by a factor of 2. At the boundary between the
convolutional and dense layers, we place a 1D dropout layer
with a zeroing probability of 0.2, followed by both a maximum
and average pooling layer that are concatenated, effectively
doubling the number of channels again. Finally, two equal
width dense layers lead to the output. Using both pooling
methods allows dense layers to learn whether a maximum or
an average is most informative for the ultimate prediction task.
All layers use ReLU for non-linearity.
B. Data Reduction
Reduction of the simulation output data serves two purposes: it reduces the cost and time required to load data from
disk, and it simplifies the NN architecture and training. We
consider data reduction in both the time sampling and bus
measurement dimensions of the simulation outputs.
1) Temporal Axis Thinning: To optimize computational
efficiency and reduce input dimensionality, we apply Temporal Axis Thinning via non-uniform sampling windows. For
a fault occurring at t = 1.0 s, the high-transient window

(t ∈ [0.95, 1.2) s) retains a fine observation resolution of
0.004 s (250 Hz) to capture rapid fault-on and immediate
post-fault dynamics. For the extended post-fault window (t ∈
[1.2, 4.0] s), where slower dynamics dominate, the observation
resolution is downsampled to 0.02 s (50 Hz, with up to 0.04 s
permissible). This reduction is physically justified by the
Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem for tracking rotor angle
oscillations, and it directly aligns with real-world streaming
data constraints defined in the IEEE C37.118 synchrophasor
standard [18].
We provide the calibration NN information on this heterogeneous observation resolution by appending a channel to the
input with the time at each sample.
2) Channel Reduction and Bus Selection: In contrast to
reducing the temporal resolution of the data as above, selecting
the most beneficial data streams for training involves more
consideration. Especially in very large grids, many of the bus
voltage measurements may be irrelevant to the parameters of
interest, and the calibration NN would require more parameters
and longer training times to effectively learn meaningful
relationships among noise. In real-world applications, this
problem may translate to limiting physical sensor placement
or collection of data from different grid entities, which can
reduce the cost of data collection.
To select relevant sensors for the NN training, we propose a method based on mutual information (MI). MI is
an information-theoretic measure of the dependence between
two random variables beyond linear relationships. It has the
potential to capture associations throughout the entire joint
probability distribution of the two random variables. We apply
MI in a greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1). Algorithm 1 is similar
to the MI-based feature selection algorithm of [19], with the
difference that Algorithm 1 can process higher-dimensional
features through the initial dimension reduction step. However,
like the algorithm of [19], it further avoids including redundant
information, as a bus Bj ′ that is strongly associated with Bj
will not add much new information to the relationship with
the parameters of interest, inertia here {H1 , . . . , Hp }. It also
avoids selecting the same bus multiple times, because the joint
informativeness of all parameters is considered.
C. Parameter Sensitivity Analysis

if certain parameters have the potential to be calibrated
more or less precisely, i.e., with less or more uncertainty,
respectively. Parameters that affect bus voltages more strongly
will be calibrated more precisely from measurements, as the
measurements contain more information about their value.

parameters most and least likely to be calibrated precisely.

III. E MPIRICAL RESULTS
We demonstrate the proposed method by calibrating generator inertia in synthetic grids. We choose inertia as a test case
because it affects the dynamic voltage response after a fault.
Specifically, varying inertia values can change the stability of
the grid response and the speed of voltage oscillations. Additionally, inertia can be changed in a dynamic grid simulation
without needing to recompute the steady state power flow
solution or change the dynamic simulation initial conditions.
In the synthetic grids, we simultaneously calibrate four inertia
parameters to demonstrate the multivariate Gaussian loss NN’s
ability to produce correlated uncertainty estimates.
The Illinois synthetic grid, ACTIVSg200, (Fig. 1) consists
of 200 buses, and is modified from the Texas A&M Electric Grid Test Case repository [24] for use in GridKit. The
modified version is available with GridKit source code. The
test case is a synthetic grid geographically located in central
Illinois, with comparable generation and load profiles. We will
calibrate four GENROU (round-rotor synchronous generator)
inertia parameters, H, out of the 40 total generators in the
synthetic grid. Fig. 2 shows an example of the synthetic PMU
measurements produced, in this case, the sin of the voltage
angle on an example bus. The differences shown between high,
medium, and low inertia are what the calibration NN will try
to capture in training in order to produce inertia estimates from
measurements.
We vary the inertia values of the generators GENROU 126 1 GENROU 135 1, GENROU 115 1, GENROU 189 1 over a Gaussian prior with standard deviation
equal to 12% of their nominal values. All four study generators
are among the top 12 according to the SA values of µ∗ (Fig.
3), so the available measurements contain information that can
be used to calibrate them precisely. Next, we select four buses
to train the calibration NN. Using Algorithm 1 and PCA as

the buses where the study generators are attached to the grid,

IV. C ONCLUSION
We presented a simulation-based inference framework that
calibrates dynamic grid parameters via a NN trained with
a multivariate Gaussian loss, yielding point estimates with
full posterior covariances. To make the method tractable for
large grids, we reduce the NN-training data along both the
temporal and bus dimensions, selecting the most informative
buses via a greedy MI algorithm. A Morris SA additionally
screens, before training, which parameters are likely to be
calibrated precisely. Results on the Illinois test case matched
the SA: sensitive parameters were calibrated precisely, while
insensitive ones honestly reverted to the prior, and residual
correlations exposed otherwise hidden parameter interactions.

The proposed method was also tested on a synthetic Hawaii
grid (Texas A&M Electric Grid Test Case repository [25]
for use in GridKit). The Hawaii grid configuration differs
significantly from the Illinois one, hence their simulations
and subsequent numerical results differ as well. However, the
Morris SA distinguished parameters with reliable estimates
from those without, and the calibration NN produced appropriate correlated uncertainty estimates. Finlly, leveraging the
generalizability of the method, future work includes extending
the framework beyond inertia parameters and to different grid
size and configurations, as well as exploiting the calibrated
posterior distributions in downstream tasks such as contingency analysis, where Monte Carlo sampling from the full
estimated Gaussian can propagate calibration uncertainty into
grid resilience studies.
